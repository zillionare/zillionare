# 20 Months of Self-Taught Quant: From Retail Trader to Risk Manager

Over the past two years, the prevailing narrative in the industry regarding quantitative finance salaries has been roughly this: in Shanghai, a junior quant’s base salary plus bonus typically ranges from 600,000 to 800,000 RMB; in Hong Kong, it’s around 1.2 to 1.5 million HKD; and if you manage to join a top-tier hedge fund in New York, the starting salary is usually $300,000–$400,000 USD.

Seeing these numbers, many people’s first reaction is: “I need to learn Python and build a machine learning model to trade stocks.”

That was exactly my mindset. As a retail investor who had paid a steep tuition in China A-shares, I believed that mastering quantitative methods and coding would allow me to precisely predict tomorrow’s market movements, leading to financial freedom.

It wasn’t until I stumbled through 20 months of self-directed learning—watching my account balance fluctuate wildly, losing and gaining repeatedly—that I gradually understood: **the core of quantitative trading is not writing code, nor is it predicting the future. It is more like a process of retraining yourself to understand the market.**

Looking back, the most valuable lessons from these 20 months boil down to a few key areas. If you are also trying to learn quantitative trading from scratch, this might help you avoid some pitfalls.

## From Guessing Direction to Calculating Probability

As a retail trader, my favorite activity was analyzing charts and guessing market direction. “This candlestick has a long lower shadow; it must go up tomorrow!”

This is typical absolute thinking. After I started learning quantitative methods, the first thing that woke me up was **probability theory**: in the market, the word “certain” is the most expensive.

### 1. Retail Traders Look at Points; Quants Look at “Conditions”
I used to think that because a company was good, its stock should rise. Later, I understood that isolated events have no trading value. What matters is **conditional probability**:

$$
P(A \mid B)=\frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

I no longer ask, “Will it go up tomorrow?” Instead, I ask the program: “Over the past 10 years, when the broader market fell below its 30-day moving average, but the stock’s net inflow exceeded 100 million RMB (Condition B), what is the probability that it rises the next day (Event A)?” Only by adding strict constraints did I realize that the “patterns” I once believed in were all illusions.

### 2. The Essence of Being Stuck Is Ignoring Bayes
What is the worst way for a retail investor to lose money? Holding on through a crash, searching for bullish news to comfort oneself.
Bayes’ theorem cured my stubbornness:

$$
P(H \mid D)=\frac{P(D \mid H)P(H)}{P(D)}
$$

No matter how bullish I was before buying (prior), if the stock breaks below my quantitative defense line today (new data), my confidence must decrease (posterior). Stop-losses must be executed. The biggest taboo in trading is stubbornness; when new data arrives, previous judgments must be adjusted.

### 3. Why Did My Account Blow Up Despite Correctly Predicting the Major Trend?
$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_i x_iP(X=x_i) \quad \text{with} \quad \mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\right]
$$
I once backtested a strategy with high returns (positive expectation) and went all-in. Then, an extreme market event occurred, causing a 40% drawdown. Terrified, I cut my losses. Immediately after I sold, the strategy started making money. I finally understood: expectation determines whether you can make money, while variance determines whether you survive long enough to do so.

**My Full-Time Self-Study Checklist (Duration: ~1 Month):**
- **Core Reading:** Sheldon Ross’s *A First Course in Probability* (Read the first 7 chapters thoroughly and treat the exercises as interview questions).
- **Practical Task 1:** Write a Monte Carlo simulator in Python to calculate the probability of ruin if a strategy has a 55% win rate, 1:1 odds, and your capital can only withstand 10 consecutive losses over 100 trades.
- **Practical Task 2:** Write a Bayesian tracking script to simulate a trend-following strategy that loses for 5 consecutive days, observing how its “posterior validity” drops below the 20% warning line.

```python
import numpy as np

# Calculate: 55% win rate, 1:1 odds, initial capital 100k, 10k bet size, ruin probability within 100 trades
def simulate_ruin(win_rate=0.55, initial_capital=10, bet_size=1, n_simulations=10000, n_trades=100):
    ruin_count = 0
    for _ in range(n_simulations):
        capital = initial_capital
        for _ in range(n_trades):
            # Win +10k, Lose -10k
            capital += bet_size if np.random.rand() < win_rate else -bet_size
            if capital <= 0:
                ruin_count += 1
                break
    return ruin_count / n_simulations

ruin_prob = simulate_ruin()
print(f"Ruin Probability for Positive Expectation Strategy (Variance Penalty): {ruin_prob*100:.2f}%")
# Even with positive expected returns, if position sizing is incorrect, you may still go bankrupt.
```

## Learning to Doubt Yourself Before Writing Strategies

After learning some coding, I started writing strategies frantically. One day, I tuned a parameter set that yielded a perfect 50% annualized return, so excited I couldn’t sleep. When I went live, I lost 15% in half a month.

At that moment, I realized that the human brain is too easy to deceive itself. I had to learn **statistics** to pour cold water on my enthusiasm.

### 1. Assume You Are a Liar
Now, when I get good backtest results, my first reaction is not joy, but hypothesis testing: Assume that this 50% return was entirely due to luck (null hypothesis $H_0$). What is the probability (p-value) of achieving this result purely by chance? If the probability is high, the strategy is garbage.

### 2. Parameter Tuning Is Self-Deception
I used to try various parameters: if the 5-day moving average didn’t work, try 10 days. I could always find a profitable one.
Later, I learned this is the multiple comparisons problem. If you try 1,000 times, you will eventually hit a lucky streak. Without Bonferroni correction ($\alpha_{\text{adj}}=\frac{\alpha}{n}$), the so-called “strongest strategy” I selected was merely overfitting a segment of historical noise.

### 3. Accept That You Cannot Predict Accurately
$$
\hat{\theta}_{MLE} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i\mid\theta)
$$
How does the market really work? No one knows. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) taught me pragmatism: take the current batch of data and reverse-engineer a set of parameters that are “most likely to generate this data” for practical use. Don’t pursue perfect predictions; just make it work for the current sample.

**My Full-Time Self-Study Checklist (Duration: ~1.5 Months):**
- **Core Reading:** Casella & Berger’s *Statistical Inference* (Very rigorous, cures all assumptions about statistical indicators).
- **Practical Task 1:** Pull 10 years of S&P 500 data, write a rolling window, and use MLE to estimate the kurtosis and fat-tail characteristics of returns.
- **Practical Task 2:** Write a permutation test from scratch: fix future returns, shuffle trading signals 5,000 times, and see where the original strategy’s Sharpe ratio ranks among these 5,000 random results. If it doesn’t rank in the top 5%, discard it immediately.

```python
import numpy as np

# Shatter your "backtest illusion" with permutation tests
def permutation_test(signal, future_returns, n_permutations=5000):
    strategy_returns = signal * future_returns
    actual_sharpe = np.mean(strategy_returns) / np.std(strategy_returns) * np.sqrt(252)

    random_sharpes = []
    for _ in range(n_permutations):
        # Fix future returns, only shuffle the correspondence between signal and returns
        shuffled_signal = np.random.permutation(signal)
        shuffled_returns = shuffled_signal * future_returns
        sharpe = np.mean(shuffled_returns) / np.std(shuffled_returns) * np.sqrt(252)
        random_sharpes.append(sharpe)

    p_value = np.mean(np.array(random_sharpes) >= actual_sharpe)
    return actual_sharpe, p_value

np.random.seed(42)
future_returns = np.random.normal(0.0005, 0.015, 1000)
signal = np.where(np.random.randn(1000) > 0, 1, -1)

sharpe, p_val = permutation_test(signal, future_returns)
print(f"Original Strategy Sharpe: {sharpe:.2f}")
print(f"Probability that shuffled signals are still not weaker than original: {p_val:.4f}")
# If p-value > 0.05, the strategy is likely just noise.
```

## Stocks Are Not Viewed in Isolation

Retail traders look at stocks one by one. But quantitative trading cannot work this way. When managing dozens of stocks, you must learn **linear algebra** to see the network behind them.

### 1. Stocks Are Interconnected
I once went all-in on 5 stocks, thinking I was diversified, but when the market fell, they all crashed. This was because their correlations were too high.
Later, I learned to use the covariance matrix $\Sigma$. The risk of the entire portfolio is actually a matrix operation:

$$
\sigma_p^2=\mathbf{w}'\Sigma\mathbf{w}
$$

I am not buying isolated stocks; I am buying the relationships between them.

### 2. Filtering Out Useless News
$$
\Sigma=Q\Lambda Q^\top
$$
Reading news to trade stocks is too exhausting. PCA (Principal Component Analysis) helped me reduce dimensions. It filters out the chaotic ups and downs of hundreds of stocks, extracting only the two or three core axes driving the broader market. Since the covariance matrix is symmetric, orthogonal decomposition is used here. By understanding the principal axes, I no longer need to stare at individual stock noise.

**My Full-Time Self-Study Checklist (Duration: ~1.5 Months):**
- **Core Reading:** David C. Lay’s *Linear Algebra and Its Applications* (Contains excellent discussions on dynamical systems).
- **Required Derivation:** On paper, use eigen-decomposition to break down the multi-asset portfolio variance formula $\sigma_p^2=\mathbf{w}'\Sigma\mathbf{w}$ down to the most fundamental orthogonal vectors.
- **Practical Task:** Download daily frequency data for NASDAQ 100 constituents, write a PCA-based statistical arbitrage backtest: extract the first few principal components, then study the residual series after stripping public factors.

```python
import numpy as np

# Simulate a 50-stock return matrix (with systematic correlation)
np.random.seed(42)
n_stocks = 50
n_days = 1000
market_factor = np.random.normal(0, 0.01, n_days)
# Stock returns = Market factor exposure + Idiosyncratic noise
returns = np.outer(market_factor, np.random.uniform(0.5, 1.5, n_stocks)) + np.random.normal(0, 0.02, (n_days, n_stocks))
centered_returns = returns - returns.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)

# PCA: Eigen-decomposition of covariance matrix
cov_matrix = np.cov(centered_returns, rowvar=False)
eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(cov_matrix)

# Sort eigenvalues in descending order
idx = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
eigenvalues = eigenvalues[idx]
eigenvectors = eigenvectors[:, idx]

variance_explained = eigenvalues / np.sum(eigenvalues)
top_k = 3
factor_loadings = centered_returns @ eigenvectors[:, :top_k]
common_component = factor_loadings @ eigenvectors[:, :top_k].T
residuals = centered_returns - common_component

print(f"Top 3 Principal Components Explain: {variance_explained[:top_k].sum()*100:.2f}% of Total Variance")
print(f"Mean Daily Volatility of Residuals After Stripping Public Factors: {residuals.std(axis=0).mean():.4f}")
```

## The Optimal Solution on Paper May Not Work in Live Trading

I calculated the perfect portfolio weights, but what if I can’t buy them in live trading? I started learning **calculus and optimization theory** to learn how to compromise with reality.

### 1. Don’t Look Too Far; Focus on the Present
$$
f(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2}(x-a)^2+\cdots
$$
Predicting the future is too difficult. The lesson from Taylor expansion is: just hedge the risk of the current small step (first and second derivatives). Don’t guess major trends; just manage current survival.

### 2. Dancing in Shackles
$$
\min_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{w}'\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mu'\mathbf{w}\ge\bar{\mu},\; \mathbf{1}'\mathbf{w}=1
$$
Real trading has transaction costs, slippage, and position limits. Convex optimization solvers do exactly this: under these rigid “shackles,” they calculate the least-bad solution that you can actually execute.

**My Full-Time Self-Study Checklist (Duration: ~1 Month):**
- **Core Reading:** Boyd & Vandenberghe’s *Convex Optimization* (Thoroughly understand KKT conditions, which are the core code for compromising with reality in optimization problems).
- **Derivation Task:** Prove the economic meaning of Lagrange Multipliers under portfolio capital constraints (i.e., “shadow prices”).
- **Practical Task:** Use `scipy.optimize` to build a long-short portfolio model, adding constraints like “maximum leverage = 1.5” and “single-period turnover < 10%,” plus a turnover penalty term, and force the solver to find a solution.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# A "dirty work" optimizer with real-world constraints
def constrained_portfolio_optimization(
    expected_returns,
    cov_matrix,
    previous_weights,
    target_return=0.03,
    max_leverage=1.5,
    max_turnover=0.10,
    turnover_penalty=0.01,
):
    n = len(expected_returns)
    
    def objective(weights):
        variance = np.dot(weights.T, np.dot(cov_matrix, weights))
        turnover_cost = turnover_penalty * np.sum(np.abs(weights - previous_weights))
        return variance + turnover_cost
    
    # Constraint 1: Weights sum to 1 (no infinite borrowing)
    # Constraint 2: Expected return must meet target
    # Constraint 3: Total leverage cannot exceed max_leverage
    # Constraint 4: Single-period turnover cannot exceed max_turnover, calculated as 0.5 * sum(|w_t - w_{t-1}|)
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1},
        {'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda w: np.dot(w, expected_returns) - target_return},
        {'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda w: max_leverage - np.sum(np.abs(w))},
        {'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda w: max_turnover - 0.5 * np.sum(np.abs(w - previous_weights))}
    ]
    
    # Real-world shackles: Single stock position cannot exceed 30%, shorting allowed down to -10%
    bounds = tuple((-0.1, 0.3) for _ in range(n))
    
    initial_weights = previous_weights.copy()
    result = minimize(objective, initial_weights, method='SLSQP', bounds=bounds, constraints=constraints)
    return result.x

# The market is not perfect, and the solver often gives you a solution full of "compromises."
```

## Realizing Volatility Itself Costs Money at the Options Level

Finally, I touched the deep end of quantitative trading: **stochastic calculus**.

### 1. Turbulence Itself Costs Money
Let $Y=f(X,t)$, where $X_t$ follows a diffusion process driven by Brownian motion. Itô’s Lemma adds a second-order term. Many people get stuck at this step when learning stochastic calculus for the first time: $(dW_t)^2=dt$.
