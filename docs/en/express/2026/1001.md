### Microstructure Explains the Failure of Short-Term Trend Strategies

A study published in July 2026 indicates that short-term trend-following strategies have failed since 2008–2009, while long-term strategies remain effective.

Using data from the SG CTA Index and 100 futures contracts, the study found this phenomenon to be sudden, speed-dependent, and heterogeneous across assets. The study ruled out capacity constraints, electronic trading, and dynamic changes in CTA and order flow, confirming that the minimum tick size after volatility standardization as the key variable.

For contracts with small tick sizes, short-term trend returns dropped to zero after 2008, while those with large tick sizes remained stable. The mechanism lies in the self-reinforcing feedback loop of trend strategies: directional trades influence market prices, thereby reinforcing the signal.

After 2008, high-frequency trading (HFT) replaced traditional market makers. Their flat inventory limits made them unwilling to absorb large directional orders, withdrawing liquidity. In small-tick contracts, the order book is sparse; after HFT withdrawal, there is insufficient depth for trend traders to execute, breaking the feedback loop and causing short-term trend strategies to fail. <sup>[1](https://alphaarchitect.com/short-term-trend-following/)</sup>

### What Institutions Say, What Media Report, What Positions Do: Who to Follow in the Three-Part Market?

When institutional statements and media narratives are treated as valuation evidence by machine learning signals, are the narrative-shapers quietly taking opposite positions?

The study separates three observable voices in the market: institutional statements (Say), media repetition (Echo), and revealing positions (Do), and characterizes the optimal trading and communication strategies of an informed institution within a linear-quadratic model framework.

The conclusion shows whether speech is worth following depends on communication costs and the degree of position revelation: when media echoes diverge from actual institutional positions, strategies following text signals systematically lose money and must switch to reverse operations. This framework separates the pricing of "what is said" and "what is done," providing testable failure conditions for text factors. <sup>[4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38545)</sup>

### Two Specialized Paths for Large Language Models to Execute Algorithmic Trading Code Generation

General-purpose language models are not weak at writing code, but they struggle to faithfully translate a natural language strategy into program logic that actually runs within a specialized trading framework, executes on historical data, generates trades, and stays true to the user’s original intent. This is the most difficult aspect of algorithmic trading code generation.

To address this goal, the work compares two complementary model specialization mechanisms, mapping strategy specifications to program logic for specialized trading frameworks, while evaluating both executability and semantic fidelity.

The conclusion points to one thing: general coding capabilities are insufficient for this scenario. The ability to actually run within a specialized framework and produce trade records is the key differentiator between specialized and general models. Due to truncation of the original abstract, specific data intervals, model scales, and backtest metrics are not disclosed. Under this theme, trading code generation is shifting from single-turn generation to iterative validation with execution feedback. <sup>[5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39420)</sup>

### Optimal Liquidation Strategy in Closing Auctions: Comparing DQN and Projected DDPG

After continuous trading ends and transitions to a closing auction, how should remaining inventory be allocated between selling pace in the limit order book and the auction phase?

The study splits trader actions into two steps: first selling in the limit order book, then submitting signed auction plans to adjust remaining positions, connecting the two phases using estimated liquidation price signals and intermediate auction feedback. Strategy learning uses deep Q-networks as a baseline, compared with continuous action methods like projected deep deterministic policy gradient and twin delayed DDPG (TD3).

Comparative results indicate that continuous action projected policy gradient methods are better suited for handling signed auction plan constraints, while liquidation price signals between the two phases and auction feedback are key variables affecting execution performance. <sup>[6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17247)</sup>

### Large Language Model-Driven Small-Cap Trading: Integrating Financial News Sentiment, Macroeconomic Indicators, and Technical Signals

This study explores using large language models to extract richer signals from financial news than fixed sentiment dictionaries, and investigates incorporating them into portfolio construction.
